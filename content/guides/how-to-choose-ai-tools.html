There are now tens of thousands of AI products, and most of them wrap the same few underlying models. The difference between a tool that saves you ten hours a week and one that quietly drains your card each month is rarely the model — it is fit. This framework is the one we use at GDTai to evaluate every tool in our directory.

1. Write down the job, not the tool

Start with the outcome you need, phrased as a job: "turn a 40-minute webinar into five short clips with captions" is far more useful than "I need an AI video tool". The job tells you the input (long video), the output (short vertical clips), the quality bar (captions must be accurate) and the volume (five per week). With that sentence you can eliminate most of a category in minutes.

2. Check whether a general assistant already does it

Before you add a specialised subscription, test the job in a general assistant you already pay for, such as ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini. These assistants now read files, browse the web, write code and analyse data. If a general assistant gets you 80% of the way with a good prompt, a niche tool must justify its price with the last 20% — usually through workflow features like templates, integrations, team sharing or bulk processing.

3. Build a shortlist of three

Use a directory such as our AI tools directory to filter by category and pricing model, then pick three candidates: the category leader, a cheaper challenger and one with a free tier. Three is enough to compare meaningfully and few enough to test properly in an afternoon.

4. Run the same real task through each

Never judge a tool on its demo. Prepare one realistic input — your own document, your own product photo, your own messy spreadsheet — and run it through all three tools. Score each output on a simple 1–5 scale for:

  • Quality: would you publish or send this with minimal edits?
  • Speed: time from opening the tool to usable result.
  • Control: can you steer tone, style, length and format?
  • Consistency: does a second run give similar quality?

5. Calculate the real cost

Subscription price is only part of the cost. Add the time you spend editing outputs, the credits you burn on retries, and seats for teammates. A simple formula works: monthly value = hours saved × your hourly rate − subscription − editing time. If the result is not clearly positive at your expected usage, keep looking. 6. Read the data and licensing terms

For anything involving client work, check three things in the terms or trust centre: whether your inputs are used to train models (and how to opt out), where data is stored, and who owns the outputs. For images, video and music, look for commercial-use rights and any indemnity the vendor offers. Teams in regulated industries should also confirm security certifications such as SOC 2 or ISO 27001.

7. Commit for one month, then review

Pick the winner and use it daily for a month on monthly billing, even if annual is cheaper. At the end, check your usage: if you used it fewer than eight times, cancel. If it became part of your routine, switch to annual billing and enjoy the discount. Put a calendar reminder on renewal dates — forgotten AI subscriptions are one of the most common sources of wasted software spend.

A quick checklist you can copy

  1. Job written as input → output → quality bar → volume.
  2. Tested in a general assistant first.
  3. Three-tool shortlist from a directory.
  4. Same real task, scored on quality, speed, control and consistency.
  5. Monthly value calculated, including editing time.
  6. Data, training and commercial-use terms checked.
  7. One-month trial with a renewal reminder.
